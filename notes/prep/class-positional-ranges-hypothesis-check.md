This note tests a three-class positional hypothesis against the comparative phonetic material currently used in the project. The central issue is whether closure consonants, fricatives, and sonorants behave differently enough in onset, coda, and geminate contexts to justify separate timing defaults (Yungdo Yun 2022; Hankamer, Lahiri, and Koreman 1989; Howell and Rosen 1983; Billington 2015; Gibson et al. 2013; Kavitskaya 2001).

The strongest support concerns closure consonants. Positional stop closure in Korean is shortest in coda position at about 103 ms, slightly longer in onset position at about 108 ms, and longest intervocalically at about 113 ms (Yungdo Yun 2022). Geminate stop closure, by contrast, clusters much higher, around 187-202 ms in comparative stop studies (Hankamer, Lahiri, and Koreman 1989). These data support a closure class with a stable positional hierarchy and a clearly distinct geminate range.

Fricatives are also distinct, but less securely normalized. The current evidence does not provide one secure isolated onset value that can simply be inserted into the table. What it does provide is a consistent directional result: fricatives are longer than comparable stops, with final fricatives exceeding final stops by about 15-29 ms and broader models adding about 28.83 ms for fricative manner relative to stops (Howell and Rosen 1983; Morley and Smith 2022). At the same time, the present evidence does not yet provide a fully matched onset-coda-geminate fricative series measured on the same acoustic basis. The safest conclusion is therefore qualitative and comparative: fricatives are heavier than stops, but their exact class defaults remain provisional.

Sonorants and consonantal glides form a third class. Spanish liquids provide the clearest onset anchor: singleton word-initial /l/ is about 89 ms, whereas liquids inside stop-plus-liquid clusters are compressed to about 62-69 ms (Gibson et al. 2013). Lopit singleton glides fall around 93-97 ms, with geminate glides around 163-167 ms (Billington 2015). Nasals are less directly isolated, but the present source base places them roughly near voiced-stop duration rather than near the fricative band (Morley and Smith 2022; Gibson et al. 2013). Kavitskaya's transition-based discussion also supports the general claim that vowel-to-sonorant and vowel-to-glide transitions tend to be longer than vowel-to-stop transitions (Kavitskaya 2001). These materials justify a lower non-closure class distinct from fricatives, even if the measurements are heterogeneous.

PROPOSED MODEL: A cautious working table may therefore treat closures as the firmest positional class, fricatives as a heavier non-closure class, and sonorants/glides as a lighter non-closure class. The evidence supports this three-way distinction more strongly than it supports one universal millisecond value for every consonant subtype.
