Based on the provided research and the classifications established in your methodology, consonants can be distinguished by their aerodynamic and temporal properties into two functional classes: **Class 1 (Stop Airflow)** and **Class 2 (Continuous Airflow)**.

### Class 1: Consonants with Airflow Stop (Stops and Glottals)
This class is defined by a complete occlusion of the vocal tract, resulting in a "silent interval" or "closure duration" between the initial attack and the release burst (Yungdo Yun 2022). In the Akkadian inventory, this includes the stops **/b, d, g, p, t, k, ṭ, q/** and the glottal stop **/ʾ/** (Buccellati 1997; Research Notes).

*   **Duration by Position:**
    *   **Onset (Word-Initial):** Word-initial voiceless stops in English average approximately **120 ms** (Yungdo Yun 2022). In Korean, initial stops have a mean closure duration of **108 ms** (Yungdo Yun 2022).
    *   **Intervocalic:** Stops located between vowels generally exhibit the longest closure durations, averaging **113 ms** (Yungdo Yun 2022).
    *   **Coda (Word-Final):** Closure durations are typically shortest in the coda position, averaging **103 ms**, due to the phenomenon of "coda neutralization" (Yungdo Yun 2022).
*   **Gemination:**
    *   **The Primary Cue:** The duration of the silent interval (closure) is the overriding perceptual cue for identifying gemination in stops (Hankamer et al.).
    *   **Multipliers:** Geminate stops are consistently **2.5 times** longer than singletons (Hankamer et al.). Singletons average **~70 ms**, while geminates range from **187 ms to 202 ms** (Hankamer et al.).
    *   **Thresholds:** Perceptually, listeners identify stop intervals shorter than **100 ms** as singletons and intervals longer than **180 ms** as geminates (Hankamer et al.).

### Class 2: Consonants Without Airflow Stop (Fricatives, Sonorants, and Glides)
This class is characterized by partial occlusion or narrow constriction, which allow for continuous airflow and the production of friction noise or resonant murmurs (Buccellati 1996; LING 401). In Akkadian, this includes fricatives **/s, z, š, ḫ, ṣ/**, sonorants **/m, n, l, r/**, and glides **/w, y/** (Buccellati 1997; Research Notes).

*   **Duration and Articulation Data:**
    *   **Intrinsic Length of Fricatives:** Fricatives are naturally longer than stops because they require a steady-state friction phase (Stephen Grossberg). Word-initial voiceless fricatives like **/š/** reach a mean duration of **171 ms** (Stephen Grossberg).
    *   **Sonorant Transitions:** In nasals and liquids, durations are often defined by the speed of the tongue's movement. For example, the nasal **/m/** has a shorter transition than **[ŋ]** because the base of the tongue is slower to move (LING 401).
    *   **Glide Thresholds:** Approximants are identified by the duration of their formant transitions. Transitions shorter than **40 ms** are perceived as stops (Class 1), while transitions longer than **100 ms** are perceived as diphthongs (LING 401).
*   **Gemination:**
    *   **Protraction:** In Class 2 consonants, gemination is modeled not as a repetition of sound but as a "protraction" or "holding" of the articulation for an additional fraction of time (Buccellati 1996).
    *   **Trading Relations:** Perceptually, listeners may hear an affricate (which begins with a stop) instead of a fricative depending on the ratio of the silent interval to the noise duration (Stephen Grossberg).
