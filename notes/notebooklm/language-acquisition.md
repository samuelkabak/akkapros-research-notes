Research findings in second-language acquisition (SLA) indicate that a language learner who relies exclusively on textbooks and written materials will face significant barriers to spontaneous speech, as the cognitive representations formed by text are often fundamentally different from the phonetic reality of the target language.

### The Failure of "Reading-Only" Input
When learners encounter a language solely through written forms, their ability to produce comprehensible speech is severely compromised.
*   **Reduced Comprehensibility:** Experimental research comparing different input modes found that "reading-only" learners were judged to be significantly more heavily accented and less comprehensible than those who received auditory input (Uchihara et al. 2022).
*   **Inhibitory Effects:** While text is often thought to support memory, studies on difficult phonological contrasts show that any form of written input can actually have an **inhibitory effect** on a learner's ability to lexically encode the distinction between new sounds (Shepperd et al. 2026).
*   **Minimum Threshold Issues:** While reading can help achieve a "minimum-threshold" of word recognition, it fails to provide the phonetic detail necessary for a learner to be understood by native speakers in a spontaneous context (Uchihara et al. 2022).

### The Danger of Orthographic Interference
Textbooks provide a "visual anchor" that often misleads the brain into applying native language (L1) pronunciation rules to the new language, a process known as **orthographic interference** (Bassetti 2024).
*   **The "Double Letter" Distortion:** A classic example found in research is that learners of English often produce a non-native long "n" sound in the word **"Finnish"** simply because they see two "n"s in the text (Bassetti et al. 2018). 
*   **Resistance to Naturalistic Correction:** These orthography-induced errors are notoriously resistant to change; research shows that even bilinguals who have lived in a foreign country for years continue to produce these errors because the "immutable nature" of the written word in their memory overrides the sounds they hear (Mairano et al. 2018).
*   **False Phonological Categories:** Relying on a textbook can lead a learner to establish phonological contrasts that **do not even exist** in the target language, creating a mental map of the language that is permanently "non-native" (Bassetti et al. 2018).

### The Necessity of Spoken Input for Automaticity
Spontaneous speaking requires "automaticity"—the ability to recall and produce sounds without conscious effort—which cannot be developed through static written descriptions of pronunciation.
*   **The Sieve Effect:** The native language acts as a "sieve" that passes only familiar phonetic information; without hearing the language, a learner cannot "attune" their brain to the language-specific details required to position the speech articulators correctly (Flege & Bohn 2021).
*   **Perceptual Precedence:** Research suggests that successful production must be preceded by a period of hearing the language to build correct mental targets; otherwise, the learner merely reinforces their own self-produced errors (Piske & Young-Scholten 2009).
*   **The Real-World Gap:** Even if a textbook describes the "exact pronunciation" of a sound, the learner lacks the auditory feedback loop necessary to convert that abstract knowledge into the physical motor routines required for speech (Flege & Bohn 2021).
