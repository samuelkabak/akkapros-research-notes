Based on the provided research, the classification of speech segments—both consonants and vowels—is heavily dependent on their phonetic type, their quantity (length), and their structural position. Empirical studies provide specific numeric ranges in milliseconds (ms) that define these categories.

### 1\. Classification of Consonants by Duration and Position

Consonants exhibit significant durational variation based on their manner of articulation, position within a word, and whether they are phonologically single or geminated.

* **By Manner of Articulation:**  
* **Stops (Plosives):** These are defined by a complete closure. In English, word-initial voiceless stops like **/p/** have a closure duration of approximately **120 ms**, while voiced stops like **/b/** are shorter at approximately **75 ms** (Lisker 1957 cited in Yungdo Yun 2022\) 1, 2\.  
* **Affricates and Fricatives:** These are intrinsically longer than stops because they involve a frication phase (Grossberg / Stephen Grossberg) 3\. In natural speech, word-initial voiceless fricatives like **/ʃ/** (171 ms) are significantly longer than affricates like **/tʃ/** (**85 ms**) (Howell & Rosen 1983 cited in Grossberg) 4, 5\.  
* **By Position (Onset vs. Coda vs. Intervocalic):**  
* Empirical studies on Korean stops show a hierarchy where durations are longest **between vowels (intervocalic)** (**113 ms**), followed by **word-initial (onset)** (**108 ms**), and are shortest at the **word-final (coda)** position (**103 ms**) (Yungdo Yun 2022\) 6, 7\.  
* **Place of Articulation:** Within these positions, the specific place of articulation also creates a durational hierarchy: **Bilabial (114 ms)** \> **Alveolar (108 ms)** \> **Velar (102 ms)** (Yungdo Yun 2022\) 7\.  
* **By Quantity (Gemination):**  
* Geminated (long) consonants are significantly longer than singletons. In Turkish and Bengali, singleton stops average **\~67–70 ms**, whereas geminate stops average **\~187–202 ms**, representing a ratio of approximately **1:2.5** (Hankamer et al. / J. Hankamer et al.) 8, 9\.  
* Perceptually, listeners always identify intervals shorter than **100 ms** as non-geminates and intervals longer than **180 ms** as geminates (Hankamer et al.) 10\.

### 2\. Classification of Vowels by Duration and Position

Contrary to the assumption that vowels lack these distinctions, empirical data shows they are highly sensitive to quantity and position.

* **By Phonological Quantity:**  
* In Japanese, a clear mental threshold exists between one and two morae (short vs. long vowels) at approximately **250 ms** (Kosuke Sugai 2017\) 11, 12\.  
* In production, short vowels in fast speech average **\~60 ms**, while long vowels in slow speech can exceed **200 ms** (Tomoko Kozasa 2002\) 13, 14\.  
* **By Position (Final Lengthening):**  
* Vowels and syllables in the **final position** of words or phrases are significantly longer than those in initial or medial positions due to **phrase-final lengthening** (Warner & Arai 2001a cited in Tomoko Kozasa 2002\) 15, 16\.  
* **By Contextual Compensation:**  
* Within a mora, vowels adjust based on the onset consonant: a **longer onset consonant is balanced by a shorter following vowel** to maintain isochronicity (Campbell and Sagisaka 1991 cited in Tomoko Kozasa 2002\) 17, 18\.

### 3\. Classification of Silent Pauses

Pausological research provides specific thresholds to distinguish between articulatory gaps and true functional pauses.

* **Pause Threshold:** A silent interval is categorized as a "pause" only if it lasts **250 ms or more**; shorter intervals are often just breathing patterns (Goldman-Eisler cited in Harwintha Y. Anjarningsih 2024\) 19, 20\.  
* **Naturalness Optimum:** For speeches to sound natural to listeners, pauses at commas and periods should be fixed at approximately **600 ms (0.6 s)** (Mingji Lin 2021\) 21, 22\.  
* **Speech Impairment:** Children with suspected Specific Language Impairment (sSLI) show significantly higher pause durations, with average pauses of **\~1,150 ms (1.15 s)** compared to **\~740 ms (0.74 s)** in typically developing children (Harwintha Y. Anjarningsih 2024\) 23, 24\.
