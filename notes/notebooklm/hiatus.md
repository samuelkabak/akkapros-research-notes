In Akkadian, the behavior of hiatus and word-boundary interactions is governed by the presence of an underlying glottal stop and the phonotactic process of contraction (sandhi).

### **1. Words Starting with a Vowel**
Strictly speaking, Akkadian words do not begin with a plain vowel.
*   **The Glottal Stop Postulate:** Scholars assume that vowels were not allowed in the word-initial position (Buccellati 1997) (152). Instead, words written as starting with a vowel actually began with a glottal stop (Buccellati 1997) (137, 153).
*   **Orthographic Convention:** In modern transcription, this initial aleph (glottal stop) is regularly omitted because its presence is assumed to be automatic (Buccellati 1997) (153). The writing system used single vowel signs (like *i*) to indicate the presence of this glottal stop before the vowel (Buccellati 1996) (461-462).

### **2. Liaison and Consonant-Vowel Boundaries (C# #V)**
The behavior when a word ending in a consonant meets a word starting with a vowel (which is actually a glottal stop + vowel) is limited but attested.
*   **Proclitic Attachment:** Certain prepositions like *ina*, *ana*, and *eli* can lose their final vowel and become proclitic (Huehnergard 2011) (698). When these occur before words beginning with vowels, the final consonant of the preposition (like the *n* in *in*) may be written as part of the following word (Huehnergard 2011) (698).
*   **Glottal Stop Deletion:** While every word ostensibly begins with a glottal stop, instances of "sandhi across word boundary" suggest that the boundary could be smoothed over (Buccellati 1997) (137).

### **3. Word Ending in a Vowel followed by a Vowel (V# #V)**
When two vowels meet across a word boundary, the hiatus is usually resolved through contraction or the insertion of a glide.
*   **Contraction (Sandhi):** Contraction across word boundaries is considered a form of free variation (Buccellati 1997) (162). A classic example is the phrase *libbi ālim* ("heart of the city"), which can be realized as the contracted form *libbālim* (Buccellati 1997) (137, 162).
*   **General Contraction Rules:** Contiguous vowels typically contract into a single long vowel (Huehnergard 2011) (610). This long vowel is usually ultralong (marked with a circumflex) and adopts the quality of the original second vowel (Huehnergard 2011) (612).
*   **Glides:** It is likely that in many cases of hiatus, a semi-vocalic glide (*w* or *y*) was present to resolve the boundary, such as *rabiyat* instead of a plain *rabi-at* (Buccellati 1996) (467).
*   **Exceptional Sequences:** Sequences of *i* or *e* followed by *a* typically resist contraction in the Old Babylonian period, though the first vowel is often shortened (Huehnergard 2011) (611).

However, the sources do provide general evidence on how **vowel hiatus and word boundaries** are handled in other language systems, which may provide relevant conceptual parallels:

*   **Avoidance of Hiatus (Mushuau Innu):** In Mushuau Innu, (Woolridge 2015) **(1032)** states that vowel hiatus is strictly avoided. This is achieved by converting the vowels /i/ and /u/ into (Woolridge 2015) **(glides)** when they occur next to other vowels.
*   **Insertion and Preservation (Mushuau Innu):** In cases where vowels are preserved across a boundary, an (Woolridge 2015) **(epenthetic glide)** may be inserted between them to resolve the hiatus (Woolridge 2015) **(1041)**.
*   **Across-Boundary Timing (Spanish):** Research on Spanish clusters examined the temporal parameters of gestures (Gibson et al. 2013) **(across word boundaries)** (C#C). The results show that the duration of a segment following a word-final consonant is (Gibson et al. 2013) **(conditioned)** by the place of articulation and voicing of that preceding consonant (Gibson et al. 2013) **(333, 339)**.
*   **Perceptual Merging (Mandarin):** In Mandarin, certain sequences that might structurally resemble hiatus, such as a glide followed by its corresponding high vowel (e.g., [ji] or [wu]), are reported to be (Liu 2022) **(perceptually indistinguishable)** from the single vowel counterparts [i] or [u] (Liu 2022) **(439)**.
*   **Consonant Deletion (Arabic):** While not specifically addressing hiatus, (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(773)** notes that North African Arabic dialects are characterized by (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(short vowel deletion)** in open syllables, which frequently creates complex consonant clusters at word junctions.
