The provided sources provide deeper **theoretical and typological foundations** for CVC as a rhythmic anchor. While raw duration fluctuates, the CVC structure functions as a discrete **phonological unit of weight (the mora)** that stabilizes timing across different languages and speaking rates.

### **Direct Evidence**
The sources provide direct evidence that CVC syllables function as a fundamental "timing slot" through several mechanisms:

*   **Rhyme Stability and Compensation:** In production studies, while individual segments (vowels and consonants) change significantly due to voicing, the **total rhyme (VC) duration** in CVC syllables remains remarkably stable. For stop consonants, the difference in total rhyme duration was found to be as low as (Morley and Smith 2022) **(122)** ms. This indicates that speakers preserve a larger temporal target for the CVC rime even when internal durations differ (Morley and Smith 2022) **(121, 157)**.
*   **Acoustic Correlates of Rhythm ($∆C$ and %V):** The standard deviation of **consonantal intervals ($∆C$)** is one of the most reliable correlates for discriminating language rhythm classes (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(607)**, (Ramus et al. 2003) **(642)**. Because CVC syllables increase the complexity and standard deviation of these intervals, they are the primary driver of the "stress-timed" rhythmic profile (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(611)**, (White and Mattys 2007) **(1017)**.
*   **Moraic Conservation:** Phonological theory demonstrates that CVC is categorically "heavy" (bimoraic) while CV is "light" (monomoraic) (Hayes 1989) **(248, 253)**. When a coda is deleted, the **mora is conserved** by lengthening the preceding vowel, proving that the rime (VC) is a fixed temporal unit that the brain seeks to preserve (Hayes 1989) **(287)**, (Alqahtani 2020) **(878, 903)**.
*   **P-Center Perturbation:** The **perceptual center (p-center)**, or the rhythmic downbeat of a syllable, is systematically shifted by the presence of a coda. Adding a consonant to a coda increases the perceived heaviness by roughly 39% to 46% as much as adding a vowel (Ryan 2014) **(708, 709)**.

### **Indirect Suggestions**
The sources indirectly suggest that CVC duration provides a foundation for rhythm through cognitive and articulatory constraints:

*   **Compensatory Sharing:** Evidence from Estonian suggests that as a rhythmic foot adds syllables, **compensatory shortening** is shared across all components to maintain foot-level timing (Asu and Nolan 2006) **(975)**. This implies that the CVC interval is a flexible target that adjusts to fit the broader rhythmic frame.
*   **Syllable Weight Shortening:** In Levantine Arabic, vowels in CVC syllables are significantly shorter than those in open CVV syllables, suggesting that the **coda shares the timing slot** of the second mora rather than simply adding duration on top of the vowel (Broselow et al. 1997) **(810, 814)**.
*   **Perceptual Switching Thresholds:** In Japanese, the **mental representation of weight** shifts from one mora to two at approximately (Sugai 2017) **(250)** ms. This provides a clear perceptual target for a "heavy" CVC unit, even if the produced duration is shorter in connected speech (Sugai 2017) **(1002, 1008)**.

### **Unsupported Extrapolation**
*   **Universal Millisecond Values:** While the sources provide specific ranges for specific languages (e.g., Japanese), there is **no evidence for a universal duration range** for CVC. Syllabic rates vary from 5.18 syllables/sec to 7.84 syllables/sec across languages, meaning a CVC "slot" is language-specific (Pellegrino et al. 2011) **(765)**.
*   **Fixed Preparation Intervals:** There is no evidence for a specific "preparation interval" between a coda and a following onset as a distinct unit of weight. The sources treat weight as a property of **moraic linking** rather than an abstract pause (Hayes 1989) **(248)**, (Campos-Astorkiza 2005) **(13)**.

### **Modeling Recommendation**
It is **more defensible to parameterize the system by syllable and rime intervals** (light, heavy, and superheavy) than by isolated consonant durations. The sources show that segment durations are highly "elastic" and fluctuate based on **prosodic boundary forces** (Morley and Smith 2022) **(185)**. Modeling based on **duration bands for timing classes** (e.g., bimoraic targets) better reflects how speakers target stable rime intervals and how listeners categorize weight (Morley and Smith 2022) **(121)**, (Sugai 2017) **(999)**.
