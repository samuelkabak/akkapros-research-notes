Based on the empirical data provided in the sources, the maximum duration for a geminate consonant—the point beyond which it may transition from a "normal" phonological unit into an unnaturally elongated sound—can be defined by perceptual thresholds and "confidence ceilings" identified in speech experiments.

### 1. The Perceptual "Confidence Ceiling" (~310 ms)
The most direct data regarding the point where a sound ceases to be accepted as a normal two-mora unit (like a standard /ss/) comes from reaction-time (RT) studies.

*   **Loss of Categorical Confidence:** In perceptual experiments manipulating the duration of moraic units, listeners categorize sounds as "two morae" (geminate) with high confidence up to a certain point. However, when the duration exceeds **310 ms**, reaction times no longer correlate with the duration, and the judgment of the sound as a two-mora unit ceases to be "automatic" (Kosuke Sugai 2017) (258, 263). This suggests that after **310 ms**, the listener likely perceives the segment as an unnatural or "excessive" lengthening rather than a standard grammatical geminate.
*   **The 250 ms Peak:** Confidence in identifying a unit as "two morae" peaks around **250 ms**. Beyond this value, the listener's brain shifts from questioning whether it is one mora to automatically accepting it as two, until it hits the aforementioned 310 ms ceiling (Kosuke Sugai 2017) (263, 265).

### 2. Contextual "Excessive" Duration (~202–240 ms)
The sources identify specific ranges where geminates or long segments are considered "markedly long" but still functional within a lexical system.

*   **The Unaccented "Excessive" Ratio:** In Japanese, when pitch-accent cues are unavailable, speakers compensate by magnifying durational distinctions. In slow speech, unaccented long vowels (2 morae) reach an average duration of **201.9 ms**, creating a ratio of **2.42:1** relative to short vowels (Tomoko Kozasa 2002) (231, 232). 
*   **Modeled "Very Long" Boundary:** Within your model's framework, durations for "very long" realizations—those used for contextual adjustments rather than lexical base forms—are modeled between **200 ms and 240+ ms** (Research Notes) (808). This represents the upper functional tier for a prosodically realized Akkadian text before moving into territory that would be perceived as a disfluency.

### 3. Production Maxima in Slow Speech (~158–202 ms)
Data from native speakers at various rates provides a "natural" upper bound for geminate production.

*   **C1 Maximums:** For native Japanese speakers in slow speech, the first part of a geminate stop (C1) is predicted to last approximately **158.6 ms** (Haiping Jia et al. no date) (288). 
*   **Closure Maxima:** In Bengali and Turkish, where closure duration is the dominant cue for gemination, natural geminate stops average between **187 ms and 202 ms** (J. Hankamer et al. no date) (309).

### 4. Pathological and Cognitive Limits (>1,900 ms)
While not specific to the internal structure of a single geminate, data on atypical speech provides a "hard ceiling" for any held interval of sound or silence.

*   **The Disruption Bound:** Pauses or held segments in suspected language impairment (sSLI) reach averages of **1,150 ms to 1,900 ms**, which are noted to drastically slow the information rate and decrease listener tolerance (Harwintha Y. Anjarningsih 2024) (19, 22, 29). 
*   **Severe word-finding delays:** Any interval exceeding **3,000 ms (3 seconds)** is categorized as "excessively long" and is indicative of severe cognitive planning difficulties rather than a normal linguistic contrast (Rebecca Bae 2015) (452).
