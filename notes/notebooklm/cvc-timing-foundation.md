Based on the provided sources, the **CVC syllable structure** (specifically the **VC rime**) serves as a more stable foundation for language timing than individual consonants or vowels. While the sources generally reject the idea of absolute isochrony (equal units of time), they provide data suggesting that speakers and listeners target larger rhythmic intervals, such as the syllable or the foot, to maintain temporal regularity.

### **Direct Evidence**
The sources provide direct evidence that the **VC rime** (the core of a CVC syllable) is a primary unit of temporal control and robustness.
*   **Rhyme Stability:** In a production study of English minimal pairs (e.g., *feet/feed*), researchers found that while individual vowel and consonant durations varied by dozens of milliseconds based on voicing, the **total rhyme (VC) duration** for stops remained nearly identical, differing by only (Morley and Smith 2022) **(201, 254)** ms. This indicates that the CVC rhyme serves as a stable temporal target.
*   **Syllable Robustness:** Syllables are significantly more robust units in speech than individual segments. In conversational corpora, **20-22% of segments** are omitted, whereas only **1% to 5.1% of syllables** are deleted (Pellegrino et al. 2011) **(955)**. 
*   **Perceptual Weight Targets:** In Japanese, listeners exhibit a clear **categorical perception threshold** for identifying a closed CVC syllable (like /tan/) as bimoraic. This mental boundary peaks at approximately (Sugai 2017) **(1224, 1236)** ms, providing a specific durational "target" for heavy syllables.
*   **Acoustic Correlates of Rhythm:** The standard deviation of **consonantal intervals ($∆C$)** is one of the two best acoustic correlates for discriminating language rhythm classes (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(772)**. Since complex CVC structures increase $∆C$, they are foundational to the "stress-timed" rhythmic impression (Hamdi et al. 2004) **(777)**.

### **Indirect Suggestions**
The sources indirectly suggest that CVC duration facilitates rhythmic isochrony through "democratic" compensatory adjustments.
*   **Compensatory Sharing:** Evidence from Estonian suggests that as a rhythmic foot adds syllables, **compensatory shortening** is "shared more equally" among all components (including the CVC rime) to preserve the foot-level timing (Asu and Nolan 2006) **(1125)**. 
*   **The Expandability Hypothesis:** This theory posits that CVC timing is the result of **competing constraints** on duration. Segments are not fixed but resist expanding or compressing past a preferred duration based on **inherent elasticity** (Morley and Smith 2022) **(163, 190)**. This suggests that "isochrony" is not a rule but an emergent property of speakers trying to distribute "lengthening force" within a syllable-level target (Morley and Smith 2022) **(163)**.
*   **Cognitive Tolerance for Anisochrony:** Human cognition has an **"entrainment window"** where sequences with deviations up to (Cutanda et al. 2019) **(37)** ms are perceived as isochronous. This suggests that CVC durations do not need to be millisecond-perfect to function as a foundation for language rhythm.

### **Unsupported Extrapolation**
*   **Absolute Isochrony:** The sources **do not support** the claim that CVC syllables are perfectly isochronous. In English, low vowels, tense vowels, and stressed syllables are consistently longer than their counterparts, meaning CVC duration varies systematically by context (Morley and Smith 2022) **(185)**.
*   **Universal CVC Duration:** There is no evidence for a single universal millisecond value for a CVC syllable. Syllabic rates vary from 5.18 syllables/sec in Mandarin to 7.84 in Japanese, meaning the "rhythmic slot" for a CVC syllable ranges from roughly (Pellegrino et al. 2011) **(127)** ms to **(193)** ms depending on the language.

### **Modeling Recommendation**
It is **more defensible to parameterize timing systems by syllable/rime intervals** than by isolated consonant durations. The sources show that segment durations are highly "elastic" and dependent on **prosodic boundary forces** (Morley and Smith 2022) **(273)**. Because the **foot PVI** (Pairwise Variability Index) is significantly lower and more stable (around 33) than the PVI for individual syllables or vowels, modeling based on larger temporal targets (like CVC heavy rimes) better reflects the "imperfect compensation" (Morley and Smith 2022) **(311)** that characterizes human speech rhythm.
