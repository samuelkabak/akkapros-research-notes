Research findings indicate that learning a language solely through written materials—such as textbooks and grammar guides—is insufficient for developing spontaneous speech because the cognitive and physical requirements of oral production are fundamentally different from those of reading.

### The Problem of Orthographic Interference
When a learner relies only on text, their brain frequently uses the spelling and pronunciation rules of their native language (L1) to interpret the new language, a phenomenon known as **orthographic interference** (Mairano et al. 2018). This visual-first approach often leads to "non-native-like productions" that are difficult to correct later (Mairano et al. 2018). 
*   **The Finnish/Double-Letter Example:** Research demonstrates that Italian learners of English often incorrectly produce a long consonant sound (gemination) in words like **"Finnish"** simply because they see double "n"s in the written form (Mairano et al. 2018). 
*   **Resistance to Correction:** Because written text is perceived as "immutable," these orthography-induced errors can become more resistant to change than errors caused by native sounds alone, persisting even after the learner is later exposed to native speakers (Mairano et al. 2018).

### The Superiority of Oral Input for Internalization
Hearing a language is a prerequisite for "noticing" its structures, which is the first step toward true acquisition (Fatemipour & Moharamzadeh 2015). 
*   **Experimental Evidence:** In a controlled study, students exposed to **"Oral Enhancement"** (hearing grammar points emphasized in speech) significantly **outperformed** those who received "Textual Enhancement" (seeing those same points bolded or highlighted in a text) on achievement tests (Fatemipour & Moharamzadeh 2015). 
*   **The Noticing Hypothesis:** While written input allows for more processing time, oral input is more effective at helping the brain "internalize" rules for active use (Fatemipour & Moharamzadeh 2015). Without input that is "noticed or attended to," successful language acquisition cannot happen (Fatemipour & Moharamzadeh 2015).

### Building Automaticity and Muscle Memory
Speaking is the most difficult language skill to master because it requires **"automaticity"** and a comfort with the "natural flow" of speech that a static textbook cannot provide (Teshaboyeva & Normuxammedova 2024).
*   **Real-Time Processing:** Techniques such as **shadowing**—the "real-time repetition of heard speech"—are necessary to train the brain to handle pronunciation, rhythm, and intonation simultaneously (Teshaboyeva & Normuxammedova 2024).
*   **Multimodal Learning:** Forcing the brain to engage with auditory details helps build "automaticity in speech," allowing the learner to move beyond memorized vocabulary to authentic interaction (Teshaboyeva & Normuxammedova 2024).

### The Danger of Fossilized Errors
Without the constant reference of heard speech, adult learners are highly susceptible to **fossilization**, where incorrect patterns become permanently "ingrained" and "resistant to correction" (Karimova 2024).
*   **Spoken Context Pressure:** Research shows that errors are much more persistent in **spoken contexts** than in written tasks (Karimova 2024). This is because "real-time processing constraints and performance pressure" make it impossible for a learner to apply textbook grammar rules spontaneously if they haven't established an auditory foundation (Karimova 2024).
*   **The Real-World Gap:** A learner who knows grammar technicalities but lacks auditory experience will often report "difficulty correcting" their errors during live conversation, citing anxiety and limited practice (Karimova 2024).
