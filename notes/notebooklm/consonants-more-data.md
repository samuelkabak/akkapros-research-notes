### 1. Fricatives and Affricates: Onset and Manner Data
Data from running speech and synthetic experiments provide specific thresholds for these continuous airflow segments:
*   **Onset Durations:** In word-initial positions, voiceless fricatives like **/ʃ/** reach a mean duration of (Howell & Rosen 1983 cited in Stephen Grossberg no date) **171 ms**, which is significantly longer than initial affricates like **/tʃ/** which average (Howell & Rosen 1983 cited in Stephen Grossberg no date) **85 ms**.
*   **Acoustic Intensity:** Sibilants are perceived primarily through their (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **friction only**, whereas non-sibilants (low intensity fricatives) require both (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **friction and transitions** for identification.
*   **Trading Relations:** Perceptually, a switch from a fricative to an affricate can be induced by manipulating the (Stephen Grossberg no date) **silence duration** and the (Stephen Grossberg no date) **noise duration**; for instance, shortening the noise duration in "ship" induces the perception of "chip".

### 2. Sonorants (Nasals and Liquids): Transitions and Gemination
Sonorants are primarily distinguished by their temporal transitions rather than silent intervals:
*   **Nasal Transition Hierarchy:** Transition durations increase as the place of articulation moves deeper into the oral cavity; **/m/** has the (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **shortest transition**, while **[ŋ]** has the (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **longest transition** because the base of the tongue moves slower.
*   **Liquid Temporal Cues:** Liquids are distinguished by the ratio of transition to steady state: **/r/** is characterized by a (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **long transition** and short steady state, whereas **/l/** requires a (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **short transition** and a long steady state.
*   **Nasal Gemination:** The physical duration of a geminate nasal relative to its singleton varies by language; for example, the ratio of geminate **/n:/** to singleton **/n/** is (Aoyama 2001 cited in Tomoko Kozasa 2005) **greater in Finnish than in Japanese**.

### 3. Glides (Approximants): Transition Thresholds
Glides are categorized almost entirely by the duration of their formant transitions:
*   **Categorical Boundaries:** Transitions (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **less than 40 ms** are perceived as stop sounds (Class 1), while transitions (LINGUISTICS 401 no date) **longer than 100 ms** are perceived as diphthongs.
*   **Trace Effects:** In some languages, unperceived short vowels leave an acoustic "trace" in the form of a (Mushuau Innu Introduction no date) **[w] glide** or the (Mushuau Innu Introduction no date) **lengthening of the preceding consonant**.

### 4. Gemination Ratios and Positional Variation
Empirical studies of quantity-sensitive languages provide benchmarks for modeling "long" versions of Class 2 consonants:
*   **Native Japanese Benchmarks:** For native Japanese speakers, the first part of a geminate consonant (C1) is predicted to last between (Haiping Jia et al. no date) **58.5 ms** at a fast speech rate and (Haiping Jia et al. no date) **143.5 ms to 158.6 ms** at a slow speech rate.
*   **Perceptual Fusion:** To perceive two occurrences of the same consonant (a geminate) rather than a cluster, a silent interval approximately (Repp 1980 cited in Stephen Grossberg no date) **150 ms longer** is required.
*   **Coda Constraints:** While geminates appear intervocalically, they are rarely stable in word-final positions; in languages like Korean, obstruents in coda positions undergo (Yungdo Yun 2022) **coda neutralization** where they are not exploded and laryngeal contrasts are lost.
