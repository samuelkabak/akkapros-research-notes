The apparent contradiction in the data regarding silence ratios and pause durations stems from the distinction between **natural conversational speech** and **controlled experimental tasks**. The sources indicate that while natural speech minimizes silence to maintain information flow, laboratory experiments use exaggerated pauses to isolate rhythmic variables or test cognitive limits.

### **1. The 54.5% Silence Ratio (Artificial Stimuli)**
The figure of **54.5% silence** is derived specifically from an **experimental synchronization task** involving children with developmental dyslexia (Marina Rossi, Eline Smit, and Tamara Rathcke 2023) **(17)**. 
*   **The Task:** Participants were asked to synchronize finger taps to a single repeated monosyllabic word (*re*) produced by a speaker (Marina Rossi, Eline Smit, and Tamara Rathcke 2023) **(17)**. 
*   **The Calculation:** The word itself had a fixed duration of **250 ms**, and the "short" pause between repetitions was set to **300 ms** (Marina Rossi, Eline Smit, and Tamara Rathcke 2023) **(17)**. This results in an inter-onset interval (IOI) of **550 ms**, where silence accounts for exactly (300/550) **54.5%** of the sequence.
*   **The "Slow" Condition:** In the "slow-paced" condition of the same study, the pause was increased to **800 ms** (Marina Rossi, Eline Smit, and Tamara Rathcke 2023) **(17)**. With the same 250 ms word, silence then accounted for **76.2%** of the sequence (800/1050).
*   **Conclusion:** These are **not natural speech ratios**. They are artificial rhythms designed to measure how easily the brain entrains to a beat (Marina Rossi, Eline Smit, and Tamara Rathcke 2023) **(13, 24)**.

### **2. The 600–680 ms Pause Range**
The range you mentioned (600–680 ms) is similarly found in **task-based environments** rather than spontaneous conversation:
*   **Paced Nursery Rhymes:** In a study of children reciting nursery rhymes to a metronome, the "inter-stressed syllable duration" (the time from the center of one stressed vowel to the next) was measured at (Ioanna Georgiadou, R. A. Knight, and L. Dipper 2015) **(440) 671 ms** (at a 1.5 Hz rate) and **525 ms** (at a 2 Hz rate). This interval includes the word, any internal consonants, and the following silence.
*   **Pre-Stimulus Silence:** In categorical perception tests for Japanese morae, a **600 ms silence** is used as a standard "waiting period" between an attention tone and the target speech sound to ensure the listener is ready to respond (Kosuke Sugai 2017) **(802)**.
*   **Cognitive Tolerance:** Human listeners perceive a rhythm as "isochronous" (regular) even when it contains temporal "jitter" or anisochrony. However, once gaps reach (Diana Cutanda, Daniel Sanabria, and Ángel Correa 2019) **(5, 7) 100 ms to 200 ms**, the brain's ability to maintain rhythmic entrainment begins to drop significantly.

### **3. The Natural Speech Baseline**
In natural, non-experimental speech, silence is treated as a "communicative pause" only when it exceeds a certain threshold, and it is otherwise minimized:
*   **The 150 ms Threshold:** In cross-linguistic studies of information rate, researchers manually identify and **discard all silence intervals longer than 150 ms** (François Pellegrino, Christophe Coupé, and Egidio Marsico 2011) **(598)**. This is because these pauses are idiosyncratic—varying widely by speaker—and do not represent the core timing of the language itself (François Pellegrino, Christophe Coupé, and Egidio Marsico 2011) **(598, 601)**.
*   **Syllabic Density:** In natural speech, English speakers produce approximately (François Pellegrino, Christophe Coupé, and Egidio Marsico 2011) **(607) 6.19 syllables per second**. This leaves very little room for long internal pauses.
*   **Micro-Silences:** Internal "glottal transitions" (short silences within words) average only (Margaret A. Naeser 1970) **(244) 18 ms**, which is nearly **35 times shorter** than the experimental pauses used in synchronization tests.
